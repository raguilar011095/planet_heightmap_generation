## 1. Classifications

### 1.1 Whittlesey 1936, "Major Agricultural Regions of the Earth"

Annals of the Association of American Geographers 26(4): 199–240. The full text was read from a university-hosted scan:
https://cbpbu.ac.in/userfiles/file/2020/STUDY_MAT/GEO/Major%20Agricultural%20Regions%20of%20the%20Earth.pdf **[V]**

**Five criteria (p. 209) [V]:**
1. The crop and livestock association.
2. The methods used to grow the crops and produce the stock.
3. The intensity of labour, capital and organisation applied to the land, and the resulting output.
4. The disposal of the products: subsistence, or sale.
5. The ensemble of structures that house and support the farming.

He also states: "Every crop and livestock association appears to have its ultimate range fixed by climate." **[V]**

**Four great groups of crop and livestock associations [V]:**
1. Dominant animal rearing in regions too arid, rugged or remote for crops.
2. Dominant crops, with animals minor or absent, and varied crops "due to a growing season never checked by cold". This is the tropics.
3. Dominant crops, with animals minor, and few crops, limited by environment or by market.
4. Roughly equal crops and animals.

**Thirteen types, plus a fourteenth: "land totally unused for agriculture" [V].** The section headings in the paper:

| # | Type | Environment and defining traits (from the text) | Pre-industrial? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nomadic herding | "too dry to produce crops, yet not utterly barren deserts"; includes reindeer herders. Water decides how long herders stay; forage is the other factor. | Yes |
| 2 | Livestock ranching | The commercial version of nomadism, run by settlers of European descent. Whittlesey says it "postdates the discoveries of the New World". | **No.** Map it to 1. |
| 3 | Shifting cultivation | Rain forest and its "bush" margins. Plots are moved every 1–3 yr and the village every 5–12 yr. Tsetse excludes large animals; the Americas had none. "Only in Africa, where the tsetse is the enemy of animal husbandry, does shifting cultivation spread far beyond the margins of the rain forest." | Yes |
| 4 | Rudimental sedentary tillage | Permanent villages inside shifting-cultivation zones: small islands, mountain valleys, unusually fertile soil and climate. Fallow is short. He includes the pre-Conquest Andean and Maya states. | Yes |
| 5 | Intensive subsistence tillage, rice dominant | "Where the season is long enough for paddy rice to mature", on deltas, floodplains, coastal plains and terraces. Two crops a year where hot. Buffalo ploughs; fish ponds and ducks; the densest rural populations on Earth. | Yes |
| 6 | Intensive subsistence tillage without rice | The cooler or drier "orient" (N China, dry India, Manchuria). The rice/non-rice border is "due to climate". Low-latitude oases such as Egypt are grouped here (irrigation). | Yes |
| 7 | Commercial plantation crop tillage | Tropical export tree crops. | No (colonial). Map it to 3/4. |
| 8 | Mediterranean agriculture | Cs climate plus hills: winter rain-fed crops, all-year tree crops, irrigated summer crops, and small livestock moved between highland and lowland (transhumance). Barley and goats in drier N Africa; wheat and sheep in S Europe. | Yes |
| 9 | Commercial grain farming | Interior temperate grasslands. | No. The pre-industrial steppe is nomadic. |
| 10 | Commercial livestock and crop farming | NW Europe and the US Corn Belt. | Partly (see 11) |
| 11 | Subsistence crop and stock farming | The subsistence counterpart of 10 (E Europe, Russia, Turkey). "Wheat almost never is found"; rye or maize is the staple, with potatoes and barley; three-field system. | Yes |
| 12 | Commercial dairy farming | Cool, humid, near cities. | No |
| 13 | Specialised horticulture | Near cities. | No |

Secondary summary with a location per type: https://anantamias.com/whittlesey-agricultural-regions/ **[S]**

**For a pre-industrial world**, Whittlesey's subsistence types (1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 11) plus "unused" give 8 readable classes. He has no forager class, because by 1936 foragers had been pushed out of the agricultural map.

### 1.4 Cultivation intensity: Boserup 1965 and Ruthenberg 1980

**Boserup, The Conditions of Agricultural Growth [S]:**
- Forest-fallow: 1–2 yr crop, then 15–25 yr fallow (sources differ, 15–20 or 20–25)
- Bush-fallow: 6–10 yr fallow
- Short-fallow: 1–2 yr grass fallow
- Annual cropping: idle only months
- Multi-cropping: 2+ crops a year on the same plot

Sources: https://eh.net/book_reviews/the-conditions-of-agricultural-growth-the-economics-of-agrarian-change-under-population-pressure/ and https://faculty.rsu.edu/users/f/felwell/www/Theorists/Essays/Boserup1.html

**Ruthenberg R value** = cultivation years ×100 / (cultivation + fallow years). From FAO (https://www.fao.org/4/r1340e/r1340e04.htm) **[S]**:
- R < 33: shifting / long-fallow
- 33–66: short-fallow
- R > 66: permanent cultivation (single or multiple cropping)

**The Ethnographic Atlas encodes the same ladder in EA028** (see 3.2): none, casual, extensive/shifting, horticulture, intensive, intensive irrigated.

**Use in the map:** intensity is best a *second dimension* (hatching, saturation, or a toggle), not extra classes. In pre-industrial terms it follows population density and water control. A simple driver: crop yield potential × accessibility to floodplain or irrigation water.

### 1.5 Livestock production systems: Seré & Steinfeld 1996; Robinson et al. 2011 (FAO/ILRI)

Source: https://www.fao.org/4/i2414e/i2414e00.pdf, chapters i2414e02.pdf and i2414e03.pdf **[V]**

This is the only classification with explicit, mappable thresholds:
- **Livestock-only versus mixed:** in livestock-only systems, more than 90 % of dry matter fed comes from rangeland or pasture, and less than 10 % of output value comes from crops. **[V]**
- **Agro-ecology [V]:**
  - Arid/semi-arid: LGP ≤ 180 days
  - Humid/sub-humid: LGP > 180 days
  - **Temperate:** "one month or more of monthly mean temperature below 5 °C, corrected to sea level"
  - **Tropical highlands:** "daily mean temperature during the growing period of 5–20 °C"
- **Rangeland is "cultivatable" only if LGP > 60 days.** "Cropping is extremely marginal in areas with less than 60 growing days, even for drought-resistant crops such as millet and sorghum." **[V]**
- Rangeland with more than 20 people/km² and LGP > 60 is reassigned to mixed. 20 people/km² ≈ 15–25 % of land cultivated. **[V]**
- Mixed farming counts as irrigated when more than 10 % of a cell is irrigated. **[V]**

### 1.6 Which classification for a pre-industrial categorical map (6–12 classes)?

None works off the shelf:
- Whittlesey's subsistence half is the closest in spirit, but it lacks foragers.
- Dixon is too modern and too policy-shaped.
- Leff is industrial.
- Boserup and Ruthenberg give intensity, not type.
- **EA042 (dominant subsistence) is the closest *benchmarkable* scheme.** It has gathering, fishing, hunting, pastoralism, extensive agriculture, intensive agriculture and mixed.
- **ArchaeoGLOBE** (foraging, extensive agriculture, intensive agriculture, pastoralism, at 1500 CE) is the closest pre-Columbian benchmark.

**Recommendation:** use Whittlesey-style subsistence types, named with Dixon-style staple words. Each type must collapse cleanly onto EA042 and ArchaeoGLOBE categories, so it can be scored. The recipe is in the summary and in section 7.

## 2. Foragers and environment

### 2.1 Binford 2001, Constructing Frames of Reference (University of California Press)

**Effective temperature** (Bailey 1960, adopted by Binford):

`ET = (18·W − 10·C) / (W − C + 8)`, with W and C the mean temperatures of the warmest and coldest months, in °C.

- **[B] for the exact form.** Marlowe 2005 is quoted with a garbled version, "18W–10C/(W − C) − 8". The form above is the standard one, and it is self-consistent: if W = C then ET = W, and if W = 10 then ET = 10 for any C. That second property is why ET 10 marks the polar and treeline boundary.
- ET runs from about 8 (polar) to about 26 (equatorial). ET 18 equals a coldest month of 18 °C (the Köppen A boundary) only when W = C.
- The generator already has 12 monthly temperatures, so ET is free.

**Thresholds (Binford):**
- **Terrestrial plant threshold, ET = 12.75.** Below it, "hunter-gatherers … cannot rely on plants as their primary food base, but depend instead on terrestrial or aquatic animal resources." Source: Stewart & Mitchell 2016, quoting Binford, https://www.originalwisdom.com/wp-content/uploads/bsk-pdf-manager/2019/03/stewart-and-mitchell_2016_Hunter-gatherer-Diversity-in-Prehistory.pdf **[S]**
- **Storage threshold: ET 15.25 or 15.75 (sources conflict).** Stewart & Mitchell 2016 write "ET ≤ 15.75 °C". One search snippet from another paper gave 15.25. My D-PLACE analysis supports roughly 15.25: the sedentary share of foragers falls from 48 % at ET 14–15.25 to 7 % at ET 15.25–17 **[E]**. Check Binford 2001, chapter 7, before quoting either.
- **Packing thresholds:** about 1.57 and 9.098 persons per 100 km². Secondary sources round them to 1.6 and 9.1: https://texasbeyondhistory.net/waco/images/population.html **[S]**. These are the densities at which mobility is constrained and intensification is forced. The exact values are **[B]**.
- **Forager versus collector** (Binford 1980, "Willow smoke and dogs' tails", American Antiquity 45: 4–20):
  - Foragers move the camp to the food, do not store, and hunt or gather by encounter. This is typical of warm, aseasonal environments.
  - Collectors send task groups out from a base, store food, and intercept resources. This is typical of seasonal, cold environments.

  Source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/abs/willow-smoke-and-dogs-tails-huntergatherer-settlement-systems-and-archaeological-site-formation/89CE96EE412C74E6CBCEA1DB904E88EB

**Binford's dataset is on D-PLACE.** It covers 339 groups with % gathering, % hunting and % fishing (B001–B003), the most important activity (B004), sedentism (B009), density (B008) and more. Licence CC BY-NC 4.0.

- https://github.com/D-PLACE/dplace-dataset-binford
- Zenodo doi:10.5281/zenodo.17580172

What the table shows:
- **No gathering-dominant group below ET 11.5.** Gathering dominates 69 % of groups at ET ≥ 12.75, which confirms the 12.75 threshold.
- Fishing dominates the coldest bands. Hunting peaks at ET 10–12.75, the continental caribou, bison and moose interiors.
- **Sedentism is a fishing phenomenon.** 58 % of fishing-dominant groups are sedentary, against 21 % of gatherers and 6 % of hunters.
- Median density is 25 per 100 km² for fishers, 12.5 for gatherers and 2 for hunters.
- Sedentary groups have a median density of 40.8 per 100 km², against 4.8 for mobile groups. Their median ET is 12.5, 66 % are fishing-dominant, and their median distance to the coast is 33 km.
- By latitude: gathering never dominates poleward of 50°. Fishing share rises from 11 % (0–10°) to 61 % (>70°).

### 2.2 Tallavaara, Eronen & Luoto 2018, PNAS 115: 1232–1237

"Productivity, biodiversity, and pathogens influence the global hunter-gatherer population density." Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5819417/ **[V]**

- Data: about 300 populations from Binford and Kelly (234 low-productivity, 66 high).
- NPP is climate-based, from WorldClim annual temperature and precipitation via the **Miami model**. The generator can compute it the same way.
- **Breakpoint at NPP ≈ 1,360 g/m²/yr.** Below it, density rises with NPP and biodiversity matters most. Above it, density is "largely independent" of NPP and pathogen stress depresses it.
- Predicted density, highest first: **temperate oceanic forest > subtropical humid forest > temperate continental forest > tropical rainforest**. The authors call the differences small.
- Model R² 0.46.
- Caveat they give: aquatic resources are not included.

### 2.3 Kavanagh et al. 2018, Nature Human Behaviour 2: 478–484

"Hindcasting global population densities reveals forces enabling the origin of agriculture," doi:10.1038/s41562-018-0358-8. **[S]**

- The model is trained on Binford's forager densities with ecoClimate climate, and hindcasts densities from 21 to 4 ka.
- In all 12 origin centres, domestication coincided with *improving* conditions and rising potential density: the "surplus" hypothesis.
- Catalogue page: https://hraf.yale.edu/ehc/documents/1289. I could not read the model details (paywalled).
- **Lesson for the generator:** farming should appear first in the most productive, seasonal, mid-latitude or subtropical places, not in marginal ones.

### 2.4 Sedentary and complex foragers: environmental signature

**The literature consensus**, from Testart 1982 (Current Anthropology 23: 523–537) and Arnold 1996:
- Large-scale storage of a *seasonal, abundant, storable* staple is the precondition. Testart calls it "necessary though not sufficient" for inequality and sedentism. **[S]** https://journals.openedition.org/paleo/6710
- Resources must be "concentrated, abundant and reliable". **[S]**

**Cases:**
- **Pacific Northwest Coast.** Salmon runs plus marine mammals and shellfish. Cool oceanic climate (Cfb/Csb/Dfc coast), ET ≈ 12–14. **[B]**
- **California.** Acorns (storable mast) plus salmon and Channel fisheries in the California Current upwelling. Cs climate, ET ≈ 14–16. Among the densest forager populations. **[B]**
- **Jōmon Japan.** Chestnut, walnut and acorn mast, salmon, shellfish (shell middens). Temperate deciduous and broadleaf forest at the Kuroshio and Oyashio front. **[B]**
- **Calusa, SW Florida.** Estuarine fisheries in shallow seagrass estuaries, around 26 °N. A tributary kingdom of more than 10,000 people, with no maize, beans or manioc. **[S]**
  - https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/sflarch/research/calusa-domain
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calusa
  - Marquardt et al. on surplus: https://core.tdar.org/document/443076/cooperation-and-coercion-geography-ecology-climate-and-surplus-production-in-the-rise-of-the-calusa-kingdom
- **Ertebølle, Denmark (about 5400–3950 BC).** Coastal and fjord fishing, oysters, seals, waterfowl; Cfb; sedentary coastal sites; adopted farming late. **[B]**
- **Also:** coastal Peru and Chile (Humboldt upwelling; "maritime foundations" of Andean civilisation, Moseley 1975) **[B]**; Ainu; Kodiak.

**Generator signature [E + B]**. A "settled fishers" class should need:
1. Coastal or large-river location. EA fishing-dominant societies have a median coast distance of 19 km (p25 3 km); Binford fishers 15 km.
2. A high aquatic-productivity proxy: upwelling, wide shelf, cool SST, estuary, or salmon-type rivers (cold rivers into a cool sea).
3. Seasonality that rewards storage: ET roughly 10–15.5.
4. No farming able to out-compete it: crop yield low, or a growing season of 4 months or less.

In warm climates, only very rich estuaries (the Calusa case) should qualify.

### 2.5 Kelly

Kelly 2013, *The Lifeways of Hunter-Gatherers* (Cambridge), compiles the second density dataset used by Tallavaara. Its findings parallel Binford's: residential mobility and range size fall with ET and with aquatic dependence. Not re-verified today **[B]**.

### 3.4 Can the EA score a generator map on an imported Earth?

**Yes**, the same way the Köppen check works: sample the generator's class at each society's coordinates and compare.

- **Primary target: EA042** (7 usable categories), mapped to the generator's lifestyle and staple classes.
- **Secondary targets:** EA040 (animal), EA029 (crop type), EA028 (intensity).
- Give "near-match" credit along the intensity ladder, for example extensive versus intensive agriculture, or gathering versus hunting.
- Use a local window, not a single cell: coordinates are approximate, and D-PLACE's own elevation for "Koreans" is 3,573 m, which is clearly wrong.

**Pitfalls:**
1. **Ethnographic present ≈ 1850–1950, post-contact:**
   - The Plains horse complex is post-Columbian. Equine-dominant societies cluster in NW and N-Central USA and W Canada.
   - Andean bovines and sheep: the Aymara are coded bovine.
   - Maize and cassava in Africa are post-1500 **[B]**.
   - Sweet potato in the New Guinea highlands: the "Ipomoean revolution", probably post-1500 **[B]**.
   - Some ploughs are coded "not aboriginal" (EA039 = 2).
   - Wherever possible, score staples against ArchaeoGLOBE 1500 CE or the Galor–Özak pre-1500 CSI instead.
2. **Sampling bias.** Africa holds 527 of 1,291 societies (41 %). North America is heavily sampled. Large agrarian civilisations (China, India, Europe) are a handful of points each. Weight by region or by area, for example by Köppen zone or ArchaeoGLOBE region.
3. **Encapsulated foragers.** Many 20th-century foragers had been pushed into marginal land by farmers (Kalahari, rainforest "pygmies", Great Basin). Binford flags 54 groups as "suspect". Do not over-fit forager zones to their modern locations.
4. **Cultural, not environmental, variables:** milking, pig avoidance, and plough diffusion. Do not tune climate rules to these.
5. **Coarse climate.** ecoClimate CCSM 1900–1949 is a GCM grid. Compute ET from the generator's own imported-Earth climate instead.
6. **One point per "society"** regardless of population or area.

### 4.1 Tsetse belt

- **Area:** about 9–10 million km² in 36–37 countries; about 37 % of Africa, or half its habitable land. About 7 million km² of it would otherwise suit livestock. In Tanzania up to 70 % of land is infested. **[V]**
  - FAO: https://www.fao.org/4/x5527e/x5527e04.htm
  - https://www.fao.org/4/y2022e/y2022e02.htm
- **Latitude:** about **15 °N** (Senegal, Niayes) to **28.5 °S** (KwaZulu-Natal) in the FAO Continental Atlas, 28 March 2025, from 669 publications covering 1990–2020. Older literature gives 14 °N–29 °S.
  - https://www.fao.org/agroinformatics/training-and-resources/data-sets/data-set-detail/the-continental-atlas-of-the-distribution-of-tsetse-flies-in-africa/en **[V]**
  - doi:10.4060/cd2022en
- **Climate:** "Tsetse flies thrive in areas with mean annual temperatures of 19–30 °C." Survival is "close to zero" after exposure above **36 °C**. Long cold winters with frost exclude them.
  - Mugenyi et al. 2021, PLoS NTD, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8648107 **[S]**
- **Rainfall and vegetation:** "vast humid and subhumid areas" **[V]**. The *G. morsitans* limit follows roughly the **508 mm isohyet** of summer-rain savanna (a MaxEnt study) **[S]**. Woody cover and shade are required: savanna (morsitans group), riverine and lake shore (palpalis group), forest (fusca group). Riverine species push north along rivers.
- **Altitude:** in Ethiopia the "tsetse-free line" is about **2,000 m** (FAO, https://www.fao.org/4/u6600t/u6600T0g.htm) **[S]**. In Uganda, abundance is near zero above about 3,000 m **[S]**. **About 50 % of the livestock in the 37 infested countries is in six East African countries, largely on highlands above the tsetse limit** **[V]**.
- **Effect:** about 30 % of 147 million cattle in affected countries are exposed. Whittlesey: tsetse restricts shifting cultivators to "poultry, pigs, and goats" **[V]**.

**Generator rule (proposed).** On any planet, a "tsetse-like" belt would be scored, not hard-cut:
- High where the mean annual temperature is 19–30 °C, the warmest month mean is under 32 °C, and annual rain exceeds about 500–600 mm with a real wet season.
- High where tree or woody share is substantial and the elevation is below about 1,800–2,000 m in the tropics.
- Fading to zero below a coldest month of about 10–12 °C or with frost.

Inside it: cattle and horse suitability falls sharply (keep trypanotolerant cattle at about 25 %); goats, pigs and poultry take over; no plough; no milking.

Other disease barriers:
- **African horse sickness** (horses only; the zone is wider than tsetse, all of sub-Saharan Africa).
- **Surra** (*Trypanosoma evansi*, by biting flies) limits camels in humid zones **[B]**.

## 5. Staples before 1492, and modern gridded crop data

### 5.1 Pre-Columbian staple geography [B unless marked]

**Old World:**
- **Wheat and barley:** SW Asia, the Mediterranean, Europe, the Nile, NW India; N China from about 2500 BC.
- **Rye and oats** in cool N and E Europe. Whittlesey's subsistence crop-and-stock farming: "Rye or maize … potatoes and barley" **[V]**, describing the 1930s.
- **Paddy rice:** S and SE Asia, S China, Japan, Korea. **African rice** (*O. glaberrima*) in the Inland Niger Delta and the Upper Guinea coast.
- **Foxtail and broomcorn millet:** N China, the steppe margins.
- **Sorghum and pearl millet:** Sahel and Sudan savanna, the Deccan.
- **Yams:** the West African "yam belt" (Guinea savanna and forest edge).
- **Enset and tef:** Ethiopia.
- **Taro, yams, bananas, breadfruit, sago:** SE Asia, New Guinea, Oceania. **Sweet potato** was in Polynesia before 1492 (pre-Columbian contact).

**Americas:**
- **Maize:** Mesoamerica to the SW USA and the Eastern Woodlands. Northern limit: Québec, with about 135 frost-free days, "practically the minimum" **[S]** (https://parks.canada.ca/lhn-nhs/qc/cartierbrebeuf/culture/autochtone-indigenous/natcul6). On the Plains, the limit of large-scale maize was the Missouri valley in North Dakota **[S]** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_agriculture_on_the_Great_Plains). Also the Andean valleys.
- **Potato, oca, quinoa:** high Andes, above about 3,000 m.
- **Manioc (cassava):** lowland tropical South America and the Caribbean.
- The Eastern Agricultural Complex.

**Post-1492 crops to keep out of a pre-industrial Earth score:**
- maize and cassava in Africa and Asia
- potato outside the Andes
- wheat, barley, cattle, sheep and horses in the Americas
- sweet potato in highland New Guinea, probably

**ArchaeoGLOBE** (section 5.3) states that in the Americas pastoralism "was restricted to its origin in the Andes … until after 1500 CE" **[V]**. Search budget ran out before I found a gridded pre-1492 staple map. The best quantitative substitutes are ArchaeoGLOBE (land-use type by region) and Galor–Özak CSI pre-1500 (caloric potential from crops available per continent).

### 5.3 ArchaeoGLOBE (Stephens et al. 2019, Science 365: 897–902) [V]

The best pre-industrial land-use benchmark.
- 255 archaeologists filled 711 surveys covering 146 regions, at time slices from 10 ka to 1850 CE, including **1500 CE**.
- **Categories:**
  - **Foraging/hunting/gathering/fishing**
  - **Extensive agriculture** ("swidden/shifting cultivation and other forms of noncontinuous cultivation")
  - **Intensive agriculture** ("all other forms of continuous cultivation (including irrigated and nonirrigated annual cropping, tropical agroforestry, flooded field farming …)")
  - **Pastoralism** ("sheep, goats, camels, cattle, horses, llamas, reindeer, and yaks")
  - Urban centres, present or absent
- **Prevalence:** none / minimal (<1 % of land) / common (1–20 %) / widespread (>20 %). Categories are non-exclusive.
- **Licence:** data **CC0**, code MIT, figures CC BY.
  - Data: doi:10.7910/DVN/CNCANQ (`ARCHAEOGLOBE_CONSENSUS_ASSESSMENT.tab`, `..._MEDIAN_ASSESSMENT.tab`, `PUBLIC_DATA.tab`)
  - Region polygons: doi:10.7910/DVN/CQWUBI (`ArchaeGLOBE_Regions.zip`, built from Natural Earth admin-1)
  - Code: doi:10.7910/DVN/6ZXAGT
  - Paper PDF: https://faculty.washington.edu/bmarwick/PDFs/Stephens-et-al-2019-10.1126-science.aax1192.pdf
- **Scoring idea:** for each region, compute the generator's area share of each lifestyle (foragers, shifting, permanent farming, herders) on imported Earth. Compare it with the 1500 CE prevalence levels, using a near-match credit of ±1 level.
- Key results: by 1850, 73 % of regions had less-than-widespread foraging. Pastoralism was "productive … across arid regions … including arid and northern regions where agriculture was unsuitable".

## 6. Method of the D-PLACE analysis [E]

- Scripts (kept in plans/food-research/scripts/; each takes the data folder as its argument):
  - `scripts/env.mjs`
  - `scripts/binford.mjs`
- Data (not kept: CC BY-NC, re-download from D-PLACE): `dl-ea`, `dl-binford`, `dl-ecoclim`, `dl-gmted`, `dl-gshhs` (raw CLDF CSVs from GitHub).
- **ET is approximated.** ecoClimate gives only the annual mean T and the variance of monthly means. For a sinusoid, the half-range A = √(2·variance), so W = mean + A and C = mean − A. Sanity checks: Kuskowagmut (AK) gives W 11.2 / C −17.7 against real values of about 13.5 / −14; !Kung gives 26.4 / 15.6 against about 26 / 13.
- Precipitation: ecoClimate's ml/m²/month × 12 / 1000 = mm/yr.
- The thresholds above should be re-derived using the generator's own climate on imported Earth. That is also how the scorer would work.

## 7. Proposed pre-industrial classification (10 classes plus "empty")

These are recipes, not code. Inputs are named as the generator has them. **Use smooth scores, not hard cuts** (owner rule): each class gets a suitability score and the highest one wins. The thresholds are where each score crosses 0.5.

Derived inputs:
- **ET** from the warmest and coldest months.
- **Wet months:** P > ~60 mm or P > PET/2.
- **Growing season months** (already present).
- **Aridity:** annual P against PET.
- **Floodplain water:** river discharge × low slope.
- **Coast productivity:** upwelling + shelf + cool SST + no ice.
- **Tsetse score** (section 4.1).
