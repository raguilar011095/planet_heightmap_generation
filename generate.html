World Orogen — Procedural Planet Generator

World Orogen is a free, browser-based procedural planet generator: believable worlds from the ground up, in a studio of planet tools that gets you from a blank page to a world that makes sense — no download or account required. Its planets are shaped by tectonic plates, erosion, volcanism and a seasonal climate, with the science behind each layer simplified but real. Three tools share one climate: Generate builds a planet from sliders, Import Heightmap runs the climate on your own map, and Evolution simulates a planet's whole tectonic history, with the rocks, ores and food its land could offer.

How It Works

Start by adjusting sliders for detail level, number of tectonic plates, continents, and terrain roughness. Click "Build New World" to generate a unique planet with realistic continental shapes, mountain ranges, ocean trenches, volcanic island chains, and hotspot volcanism. Every planet is procedurally generated and completely unique.

Key Features

  • Tectonic plate simulation with convergent, divergent, and transform boundaries
  • Every planet finished with a past: rivers carved by its climate's runoff, lakes, a last ice age, dunes and loess, a rock map, ore, fuel and gem deposits, and what its land could feed
  • Climate simulation with seasonal wind patterns, ocean currents, precipitation, and Köppen climate classification
  • Hotspot volcanism with drift-trail island chains (like Hawaii)
  • Interactive plate editing — select multiple plates for batch land/ocean toggling with visual preview before rebuild
  • Heightmap import — bring your own equirectangular B&W heightmap (Earth, Mars, hand-drawn) and run full climate simulation on it
  • Evolution — watch a planet's continents rift, drift and collide over a billion years, then sculpt any moment in detail with rivers, glaciers, its own climate, a satellite view and a geology view: a rock-type map, ore, fuel, mineral and gem deposits built from that history — each district with the story of how it formed — what each technological age could mine, and exports of them all (maps, richness masks, GeoJSON, a resource atlas) — and a food view (what its land could grow, what each place would live on most, and whether its people would farm, herd or gather); and save the planet as a file to open again later
  • Temperature and precipitation controls that work through the climate: cool a planet and its ice spreads, warm it and it grows wetter
  • Multiple visualization modes: terrain with rivers and lakes, a satellite view with clouds in summer and winter, climate, geology, food, heightmap and plates
  • Detailed layers for the climate and the world's past — pressure and wind, ocean currents, upwelling, sea ice, temperature, precipitation, aridity, growing season and more, for the year, January or July
  • Map export up to 16,384px wide: every map as colour or as 16-bit data on documented scales (heightmaps, hillshade, temperature, rain, winds, currents, ice, Köppen classes, rocks, deposits, food), in ten map projections, one PNG or a ZIP with a README
  • Shareable planet codes — copy a code to share your exact planet with others

Use Cases

World Orogen is used by worldbuilders, game developers, tabletop RPG players, fantasy authors, and anyone who needs realistic terrain. Export heightmaps for use in game engines like Unity or Unreal, or download satellite-style maps for your D&D campaign setting. The climate simulation produces realistic biome distributions for believable fantasy worlds.

Share Your World

Every planet has a unique planet code. Share the URL (e.g. orogen.studio/generate#CODE) to let anyone recreate your exact world — including any tectonic plates you've edited by hand.

Welcome to World Orogen

Procedural planets inspired by real tectonics, erosion, and climate.

Every world is one of a kind — with believable continents, mountains, ocean trenches, and volcanic islands. Build one in seconds, tweak it until it's yours.

Shape Your World

Use the Shape Your World sliders to control detail, plates, continents, and roughness, then hit Build New World. The Detail slider only changes rendering resolution — continent shapes stay the same, so you can iterate quickly at low detail and crank it up when the planet looks good. Expand Terrain Sculpting to fine-tune erosion and smoothing — these can be reapplied instantly with the ↻ button.

The planet shows in seconds; a few seconds later its climate, rivers and lakes, ice age, rocks and food are worked out in the background, and the Satellite, Climate, Geology and Food views open.

Explore & Edit

Drag to rotate the globe. Scroll to zoom in and out. Ctrl-click plates (⌘-click on a Mac) to mark them for reshaping — select multiple, then hit Rebuild to apply all at once. Ctrl-click again to undo a pending selection. Hover any place to see its plate and crust, height, river or lake, climate — and on the Geology and Food views its rock, ores and what the land could feed.

Visualize Your World

Switch between Terrain, Satellite, Climate, Geology, Food, Heightmap and Plates with the tabs over the planet. Climate opens its own row of maps — Köppen, temperature, rain, wind, currents, and under More… pressure, sea ice, aridity, growing season and more — each for the year, January or July (the Satellite view too); Geology its rocks, deposits and what each technological age could reach; Food its farmland, staple foods, ways of life and people. The arrow at the tabs' left opens the view options above them: Globe or Map, the map in any of ten projections, its centre, the grid, the terrain's Colours, the hillshade, the clouds and the rivers; the world's past is in Inspect (advanced) in the sidebar.

Save & Share

Every planet gets a unique planet code shown below the Build button — including any plates you've edited. Copy it to share with others, or paste a code and click Load to recreate someone else's world. You can also share the URL directly.

Use Export Map to save any map the page draws — the terrain, satellite (with clouds or without), climate zones, every climate layer, rivers and lakes, heightmaps, a hillshade, plates, rocks, deposits, food — as Colour (as shown) or as Data (16-bit greyscales on fixed scales, for other tools), for the year, January or July, in ten map projections, centred where you like. One map saves as a PNG; several as a ZIP with a README that explains every file.

What's New in World Orogen

Evolution is out of beta, with two new tabs that are in beta themselves: Geology, with the rocks, ores and gems a planet's history left and what each technological age could mine, and Food, with what its land could grow and how its people would live. Histories now start from an older, pieced-together world, and zoomed in, the land is drawn finer and shaped by the rock beneath it.

Heads up: Evolution builds its histories in a new way, so a saved Evolution link or seed now grows a different planet. Links and planet files made before still open as they were at orogen.studio/v/2.1/evolution. Generate and Import are unchanged: planet codes give the same worlds as before.

Geology: Rocks, Ores and Ages Beta

Add detail to a moment in Evolution, and the new Geology tab reads its rocks and ores off the simulated history.

  • Rocks — Sediment from old seas and basins, volcanic arcs, flood basalts, granite, slate and gneiss, ancient shields with their greenstone belts. Hover a rock for how it came to be there.
  • Deposits — About thirty kinds of ore, fuel and mineral, placed by the history, the climate and the rivers: copper and gold over volcanic arcs, scarce tin far inland, nickel under flood basalts, diamonds on ancient cratons, coal, oil and gas, salt. Each is poor, moderate or rich; see them all together, one metal at a time, or by the kind of deposit.
  • Ages — What a people of the Stone, Copper, Bronze, Iron and Industrial Ages could reach, plus fuel, gems and building stone, and where bronze and iron could be made.
  • Districts — Hover a deposit for its district, how it formed and its rank on the world. Every map exports, with the districts as GeoJSON and CSV and a resource atlas in words.

Food: What the Land Could Feed Beta

The new Food tab, also on a detailed moment, works out what the land could grow from its soil, its ground and its growing season.

  • Farmland — Counted in harvests a year, by rain alone or with water brought by flood farming and canals, plus terraces and raised fields. Switch each technique on and off in the legend.
  • Staple food — What each place would live on most: paddy grains, cool- or warm-season grains, roots and tubers, livestock, fish or wild foods.
  • Lifestyle — Whether its people would farm, herd or gather. Gatherers keep their way of life unless farming or herding would feed ten times as many, so a rich coast can stay theirs.
  • People — How many the land could feed before industry: a few gatherers on the tundra, over a hundred a km² on the richest river plains. Soil and Growing season have views of their own.

Geology and Food are new and in beta, and haven't been tried on a phone yet.

Histories with Deeper Roots

Evolution's histories now start from an older world, and its collisions reach further.

  • A first supercontinent with a past — Pieced together from the ancient cores of earlier continents, with the seams where they joined and young belts along its edges. Its lowlands carry old sediment, and its old ranges are worn down by age.
  • Pushes felt far inland — A collision's push travels deep into the continent: the ancient cores pass it on and weak lines take it up, raising ranges far from any plate edge, and broad swells and basins that steer the big rivers.
  • Old ranges rise again — Where a push lands on an old range, the range lifts again along its own lines.
  • Rivers that reach the sea — Lakes form only in real hollows, and a major river ends only in a huge one, so rivers wander less and fewer end in small lakes.

Finer Detail, Shaped by the Rock

Zoom in on a detailed moment and the land is drawn finer than its mesh, at your screen's resolution, and in large exports too.

  • Up close — Faint ridges and valleys on the mountains, dunes in the sand seas, coves along the coasts. Ridgelines break up by the kind of range, and the main range by the plate edge stays most whole.
  • Hard rock and soft — Hard rock is left standing and soft rock worn into vales; on the coast, hard rock juts out as headlands and soft rock wears back into bays. Layered rock steps into benches and mesa rims, the last ice age leaves ice-scoured knobs, and limestone sinks into closed hollows.
  • Old ranges keep their grain — Long, even ridges run along the range, and rivers settle into the valleys between them.
  • Flood basalts — Flat lava plateaus like the Deccan's, with edges that thin out, escarpments where they stand above lower ground, and canyons only where big rivers cut them.
  • Bedrock from space — In the Satellite view, bare ground shows its rock's colour: red sandstone, pale limestone, white salt, dark slate, pink-grey granite.
